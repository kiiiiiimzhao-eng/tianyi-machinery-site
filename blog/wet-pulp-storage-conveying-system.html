Wet Pulp Storage Conveying System: Moving Sticky, High-Moisture Pulp in a Pulp & Paper Mill Upgrade

📅 October 13, 2026 ⌚ 16 min read ✎ Tianyi Engineering Team

Most conveying projects move dry bulk: rock, coal, cement, fertiliser. Wet pulp is the opposite of all of them. It is heavy with water, cohesive, stringy and inclined to stick to whatever it touches — and it still has to be moved in hundreds of tonnes a day, without spilling, without blocking and without turning the mill floor into a wet, slippery mess. This article is a case study of a wet pulp storage conveying system built for a capacity-upgrade project at a large pulp and paper enterprise in China, where the existing deinked-pulp line was converted into a 200,000 t/y high-yield chemi-mechanical pulp (CMP) line and a new 450,000 t/y uncoated cultural paper line was added. The wet pulp storage equipment covered in these photographs has now entered load-run commissioning — the stage at which the system runs with real material and proves that the design works.

1. The Project: A Pulp and Paper Capacity Upgrade

The project is a comprehensive technical transformation of an existing pulp and paper mill in China, carried out inside a working site. It has two main process elements, and the wet pulp storage sits between them as the material link.

ElementScopeRelevance to conveying
High-yield pulp lineAn existing 200,000 t/y deinked-pulp line rebuilt into a 200,000 t/y, cleaner high-yield chemi-mechanical pulp (CMP) lineProduces the wet pulp that the storage system has to receive, distribute and buffer
Cultural paper lineA new 450,000 t/y intelligent uncoated (cultural) paper line with its stock preparation hallConsumes the pulp; the storage system has to supply it at a steady rate
Wet pulp storageRebuilt storage capacity serving the CMP line, with its own conveying and distribution equipmentThe subject of this article — the buffer between pulp production and papermaking
  • A working site: the transformation takes place around plant that is already in operation, so new equipment has to be installed and commissioned without stopping the rest of the mill.
  • A large, public project: the upgrade is one of the larger pulp and paper investments of recent years, with a total value measured in billions of renminbi and a construction programme of well over a year.
  • The storage is not a warehouse — it is a process buffer: wet pulp cannot be simply stockpiled outdoors. It has to be received, distributed, and reclaimed in a controlled way, or the pulp line and the paper machine have to slow down to match each other.

Wet pulp storage is a typical example of a job that looks like "just another conveyor" on a layout drawing and turns into a design problem the moment the material properties are taken seriously. That is what the rest of this article is about.

2. Why Wet Pulp Is the Hardest Material on the Site

Dry bulk materials are, in engineering terms, reasonably well behaved: they flow, they have a stable angle of repose, and they can be dropped from a chute without much drama. Wet pulp breaks almost every one of those assumptions. It arrives from the dewatering and pressing stages as a high-moisture, cohesive mass — typically half water or more — not as a free-flowing granule.

PropertyWet pulpConsequence for the conveyor
MoistureVery high; the load is part waterHeavy belt loading, water carryback, dripping and leachate, corrosion
Cohesion / stickinessAdheres to steel, rubber and itselfBuild-up on chutes, idlers and the return belt; poor release at the head pulley
Fibrous, stringy structureLong fibres, flakes and lumpsMaterial hangs in chutes and wraps around rotating parts; narrow gaps clog
CompressibilityBulk density changes with handling, mats under pressureCapacity cannot be estimated from a single bulk-density figure
Friction on a wet beltLow — the load slides easilyLimits the maximum incline; a smooth belt will not hold a load up a steep slope
Temperature and biologyWarm and moist, promotes corrosion and slimeMaterials selection, washdown and drainage become design requirements, not housekeeping afterthoughts
The rule that governs everything else: wet pulp is not a "slightly damp" version of a dry bulk solid. Its angle of repose, its friction on steel and rubber, and its ability to stick to a surface are all different properties that have to be measured and designed around. A conveyor sized for dry pulp of the same tonnage will under-perform and block.

3. System Architecture: From the Pulp Line to the Storage Bays

The storage system follows the process rather than a straight line. In outline:

Pulp dewatering and pressing → receiving conveyor → inclined flight → transfer tower → distribution conveyor over the bays → discharge into storage → reclaim to stock preparation.

  • Receiving: wet pulp discharged from the pulp line is fed onto the first conveyor flight. The feed point is designed for a cohesive mass rather than a stream of granules, with a wide, steep, lined throat and no ledge where pulp can settle.
  • Inclined conveying: the pulp is lifted to the roof level of the storage hall on an inclined belt flight. The maximum angle is set by the low friction of wet pulp on the belt, not by a standard catalogue figure.
  • Transfer tower: the change of direction between flights takes place in a structure that houses the chute, the discharge hood and the maintenance access — the steel-framed structure shown in Figure 2.
  • Distribution over the bays: one horizontal conveyor runs the length of the storage hall above the bays. Discharge devices along it — plough dischargers, a travelling tripper or fixed diverter chutes — drop the pulp into whichever bay is being filled.
  • Storage and reclaim: the bays buffer the difference between the steady output of the pulp line and the varying demand of the paper machine; reclaim equipment returns the pulp to stock preparation.
Why the storage exists at all: a high-yield pulp line and a paper machine rarely run at exactly matched rates, and either can stop. Without a buffer, a short stop on the paper machine forces the pulp line down, and a short stop on the pulp line starves the machine. The wet pulp storage decouples the two — and the conveyor system is what makes that buffer usable.

4. Belt Conveyor Engineering for a Sticky, Wet Load

A wet pulp conveyor looks like a conventional belt conveyor from a distance, and is engineered differently in almost every detail. The parameters that matter most are set out below.

ParameterEngineering consideration for wet pulp
Belt surface and coverThe cover is chosen for wet release — a smooth, non-absorbent surface that the load leaves cleanly at the head pulley. On inclined flights, where the low friction of wet pulp would let the load slide back, a cleated, ribbed or pocketed belt is used to carry the load positively.
Belt width and speedWidth is set from tonnage, the real bulk density of the cohesive load and the lump size. Speed is kept moderate: faster belts throw more water, mistrack more easily and drive more carryback, all of which are worse with wet pulp than with dry bulk.
Troughing and transitionsDeep troughing keeps a cohesive load centred, but the belt must not be forced into a tight transition — wet pulp needs generous transition distances and a large head pulley so the load releases rather than folding back onto the belt.
Skirt boards and sealingFull-length, rubber-skirted loading zones with sealed edges keep the wet load on the belt. Sealing matters more here than with dry bulk, because leakage is not dust but a continuous trickle of pulp and water.
Belt cleanersPrimary doctor blades plus a secondary rotary brush remove the film of pulp that wet belts carry back. Cleaners are stainless and set to a load that removes the film without grooving the belt; on wet pulp they are a production component, not an accessory.
Drives and startingMotor and gearbox sized for the loaded condition and for a heavy, wet start. Variable-speed control lets the conveyor match the delivery rate of the pulp line, avoiding surging and spillage at the feed point.
Take-up and trackingA take-up with adequate travel to hold tension over the belt's life, plus self-aligning idlers and training idlers to keep a wet, off-centre load tracking straight.
Materials and finishWetted parts in stainless steel or with a suitable coating, no sharp internal corners, and drainage provided so water does not stand in the structure.

The engineering effort concentrates at three places: the feed point, where a cohesive mass has to enter the belt without bridging or spilling; the inclined flight, where friction decides whether the load stays on the belt; and the head pulley, where the load has to release cleanly instead of riding back on the return belt.

5. Distributing One Stream into Many Storage Bays

The core problem of a storage system is distribution: one conveyor arrives with the pulp, and it has to be able to deliver it to a number of different bays, in any order, without a machine per bay. Three devices do this work, and the right choice depends on the layout and the operating pattern.

  • Plough dischargers (fixed, one per bay). A plough rides on the belt and deflects the load sideways off the carrying side into a chute above the bay. The plough can be fixed or lifting (manually, pneumatically or electrically), so a bay can be bypassed when it is full. Ploughs are simple and robust, and they suit a line of bays served at known intervals — but the blade has to be designed to clear a sticky load off the belt, and the discharge opening has to be large enough that wet pulp cannot bridge across it.
  • Travelling tripper. A tripper is a movable discharge station that travels along the conveyor and can be parked at any point. It is the right choice when the fill point has to move continuously, or when there are more bays than a fixed set of ploughs can economically serve. On wet pulp a tripper needs a wide discharge chute, steep angles and its own cleaning devices, because a tripper that cannot empty itself becomes the blockage.
  • Diverter chutes (multi-way). A fixed chute with a flap or a rotating spout splits one stream between two, three or four fixed destinations — the multi-way discharge hood in Figure 4. They suit a small number of fixed destinations and are the simplest solution where the process, rather than the bay layout, decides where the pulp goes.
DeviceBest forWatch out for
Plough dischargerA line of bays, filled one at a time, with a bypass positionBlade cleaning on a sticky belt; discharge opening large enough to avoid bridging
Travelling tripperContinuous or frequent changes of fill point; many baysSelf-cleaning of the tripper chute; tracking of the belt through a moving station
Multi-way diverter chuteTwo to four fixed destinations chosen by the processGenerous throat, steep angle and low-friction lining to keep wet pulp moving

The choice on this project followed those rules: a fixed set of discharge points along the distribution conveyor for the bays, and multi-way chutes where a single stream had to be split between fixed process destinations.

6. Transfer Points, Chutes and Getting the Load Off the Belt

With dry bulk, transfer points are where dust is generated. With wet pulp, they are where the line blocks — and where the belt fails to give up its load. The measures that matter are mostly the opposite of those used for dry, dusty materials.

  • Steep, smooth, lined chutes. A chute for wet pulp is set well above the material's sliding angle on the chute surface, with a low-friction lining so that the required angle comes down and the pulp cannot cling. The classic dry-bulk "rock box" that protects a chute by holding a bed of material will simply fill with pulp and block, so it is not used.
  • No ledges, no dead pockets. Every internal corner, step and horizontal face is a place where wet pulp accumulates until it becomes a plug. Chutes are built with continuous, steep surfaces and generous openings, and with large inspection doors so a build-up can be cleared quickly.
  • Positive release at the discharge. The head pulley is large, the discharge trajectory is shaped so the load leaves rather than folds back, and a scraper cleans the belt after the discharge point. Getting the load off the belt is the single most important detail on a wet pulp conveyor.
  • String and wrap control. Fibrous material wraps around rotating parts and hangs off edges. Gaps around pulleys, idlers and bearings are minimised or guarded so that strings cannot start a wrap that damages the belt or the bearing.
  • Blockage detection. Because a wet-pulp chute can block quickly, level and motion probes at critical transfer points trip the feeding conveyor the moment flow stops, before the blockage backs up into a fall of material or buries the belt.

7. Water, Housekeeping and Corrosion

A wet pulp system continuously produces water: free water draining from the load, water thrown off at the head pulley, and the film of pulp that cleaners scrape from the belt. If that water is not collected deliberately, it ends up on the floor, under the structure and inside the drive units. On this project the water was designed for, not cleaned up afterwards.

  • Drip trays and collection gutters under the head pulley, the skirted loading zone and each transfer point, draining to a collection point instead of onto the floor.
  • Sloped and drained floors around the storage bays and transfer structures, with washdown points, so the area can be kept clean without water pooling under the equipment.
  • Material selection for a wet environment: stainless steel or a protective coating on wetted parts, hot-dip galvanising or a suitable paint system on the structure, sealed bearings, and attention to cut edges, fasteners and joints — the places water reaches first.
  • Removable covers and inspection doors over chutes and loading points, so that a build-up can be inspected and cleared without dismantling the conveyor.
  • Corrosion and biological growth: because the pulp is warm and moist, surfaces that stay wet are prone to slime and accelerated corrosion. Design that drains, slopes and dries is worth more than any coating.

8. Structure, Access and Safety Devices

The distribution conveyor runs above the storage bays, which means almost all of the maintenance work happens at height and over water. The structure and the access around it are therefore part of the safety design, not decoration.

  • Walkways with handrails and toe boards along the distribution conveyor and the inclined flight, so that inspection, cleaning and belt repairs can be done from a protected platform rather than from a ladder or a mobile lift.
  • Stairs and platforms at the drive stations, transfer towers and the tripper or plough positions, built for the tools and the spare parts that have to reach them.
  • Guarding around all rotating parts — pulleys, couplings, drive shafts — and around the plough or tripper mechanism, where a moving blade and a moving belt meet.
  • Lighting along the walkway and at each inspection point, because most of the maintenance on this conveyor is visual.
  • Safety devices across the system: belt misalignment switches, belt-slip and speed monitoring, blocked-chute detection, pull-cord emergency stops along the walkway, and emergency stop buttons at every station.
  • Interlocks: the start and stop sequences are interlocked with the pulp line upstream and the reclaim system downstream, so that a stop anywhere in the chain does not leave material stranded in a chute or on a belt.

9. Load-Run Commissioning: What "Running with Material" Proves

The equipment covered in these photographs has now entered load-run commissioning — in Chinese practice, the point at which a conveyor system is run with real material rather than empty. It is the milestone that separates a mechanically complete system from a proven one, and on a wet pulp line it is where the design is really tested.

Commissioning a wet pulp system runs in two stages:

  1. No-load running proves the mechanical basics: the belt tracks, the drive turns the right way, the take-up moves, the scrapers are set to the belt, the safety devices trip when they should, and nothing fouls.
  2. Load running proves the system as a material-handling machine. Material is introduced and the following are observed until the line is stable:
  • Belt tracking under load: a loaded, wet belt does not sit where an empty belt sits, so tracking is re-checked and corrected at working load.
  • Tension and take-up travel: the belt must hold tension at full load and full speed without the take-up running out of travel.
  • Discharge at every bay: each plough or diverter position is tested to confirm that the pulp actually leaves the belt and clears the chute, rather than clinging to the blade or bridging the opening.
  • Chute flow: every transfer is watched for build-up and blockages, and the chute angles and linings are adjusted if the pulp hangs.
  • Drive load: motor current at full load is compared with the design figure, confirming the conveyor has the margin it was specified with — not less.
  • Cleanliness: carryback, dripping and spillage are checked along the whole line; if the belt cleans badly, the cleaner setting or the belt surface is the first thing to review.
  • Start and stop under full load: the conveyor is stopped and restarted full, to confirm the drive can restart a loaded belt and that an inclined flight holds its load.
  • Interlocks and emergency stops: the sequence with the pulp line and the reclaim system is tested, and every emergency stop is proved under running conditions.
  • Product condition: the pulp is checked after conveying, to confirm the system neither dewatered it excessively nor contaminated it.
Why load running matters so much on wet pulp: the problems that only appear with material — a chute that hangs, a belt that mistracks once it is wet and loaded, a cleaner that leaves a film, a plough that will not clear — cannot be found on an empty conveyor. Load-run commissioning is how they are found and fixed before handover, instead of during production.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a wet pulp storage conveying system?

A wet pulp storage conveying system receives wet pulp from the pulp line, lifts and transfers it to a storage hall, distributes it into individual storage bays and later reclaims it to stock preparation. It is a process buffer: it decouples the output of a pulp line from the demand of a paper machine, so that a short stop on either side does not force the other to slow down.

Why can't wet pulp be carried on an ordinary belt conveyor?

Because wet pulp is cohesive, stringy and wet, its properties are different from those of a dry bulk solid. It sticks to belts and chutes, hangs in openings, wraps around rotating parts and has low friction on a wet belt, which limits the incline the belt can climb. An ordinary dry-bulk conveyor sized for the same tonnage will spill, mistrack and block. The belt surface, chute angles, skirt sealing, cleaning devices and incline all have to be designed for the material.

How is wet pulp distributed from one conveyor into several storage bays?

Either by plough dischargers spaced along the conveyor, by a travelling tripper that moves to the bay being filled, or by fixed multi-way diverter chutes where the process, rather than the bay layout, decides the destination. Ploughs suit a line of bays filled one at a time with a bypass position; a tripper suits frequent or continuous changes of fill point; and diverter chutes suit a small number of fixed destinations. All of them need large, steep, low-friction openings that a cohesive wet pulp cannot bridge.

What does load-run commissioning prove for a wet pulp conveyor?

Load running introduces real material and tests the system as a material-handling machine, not just a machine that turns. It confirms belt tracking under load, belt tension at full load and speed, that the pulp actually clears the belt and every chute, that the drive has its design margin, that there is no carryback or spillage, that a loaded conveyor can be stopped and restarted, and that the interlocks and emergency stops work. Problems that only appear with material — a hanging chute, a plough that will not clear, a film left on the belt — are fixed before handover rather than during production.
